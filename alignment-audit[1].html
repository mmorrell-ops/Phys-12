q04 · Describe the photoelectric effect in terms of the photon.
d4:35 · qa
Why does increasing the intensity of light below the threshold frequency still eject no electrons?
Because energy arrives in discrete photons and one photon interacts with one electron. Raising intensity delivers more photons per second, but each still carries less than the work function, so none can free an electron.
d4:36 · qa
What happens to the number of photoelectrons and their maximum kinetic energy when the frequency of incident light is increased above threshold at constant intensity?
Maximum kinetic energy increases, because each photon carries more energy. The number of photoelectrons falls slightly, since constant intensity delivered in larger quanta means fewer photons arrive per second.
Watch for: Intensity is energy per second, not photons per second. Higher-energy photons at the same intensity means fewer of them.
d4:37 · qa
Explain why the photoelectric effect supports a particle model of light rather than a wave model.
A wave would deliver energy continuously and spread across the whole surface, so any frequency should eventually eject electrons after a delay, and brighter light should give faster electrons. Neither is observed. Emission is instant, requires a threshold frequency, and electron energy depends on frequency alone — all of which follow if light arrives as discrete quanta absorbed one at a time.
d4:38 · cloze
In the photoelectric effect, the frequency of the light determines whether electrons are emitted and how energetic they are, while the intensity determines how many are emitted.
This single distinction accounts for most photoelectric marks. Sort the variable first, then answer.
d4:39 · qa
Two metals are illuminated with identical light and both emit photoelectrons. The first emits electrons with greater maximum kinetic energy. What does this tell you about the two metals?
The first metal has the smaller work function, and therefore the lower threshold frequency. With E_k = hf - W and hf identical, less energy spent escaping leaves more as kinetic energy.
q07 · Compare the different models of the atom proposed by Rutherford and Bohr.
d4:40 · qa
Compare the Rutherford and Bohr models of the atom.
Both place a small dense positive nucleus at the centre with electrons around it. Rutherford left the electron orbits unrestricted, so the model predicts radiating electrons and collapsing atoms. Bohr restricted electrons to discrete stationary orbits in which they do not radiate, which explains both atomic stability and the discrete line spectrum.
d4:41 · qa
What experimental evidence led to the Rutherford model?
Alpha particle scattering. Most alphas passed through the foil undeflected, but a very small fraction rebounded at large angles, which requires the positive charge and nearly all the mass to be concentrated in a tiny central nucleus.
d4:42 · qa
Which observation does the Bohr model explain that the Rutherford model cannot?
The discrete line spectrum of hydrogen. Quantised energy states mean only certain transition energies are possible, so only certain wavelengths appear. Rutherford's model predicts a continuous spectrum and an unstable atom.
q03 · Explain the concept of black-body radiation and the significance of the evidence it provides.
d4:43 · qa
How does the black-body curve change as temperature increases?
The peak shifts to shorter wavelength, following _max = bT, and the total emission across all wavelengths rises. The curves for different temperatures never cross.
s07 · Contrast the fundamental forces experienced by quarks and leptons.
d5:15 · qa
Which fundamental forces act on quarks, and which act on leptons?
Quarks experience all four — strong, weak, electromagnetic and gravitational. Leptons experience the weak and gravitational forces, and the electromagnetic force only if they carry charge. No lepton experiences the strong nuclear force.
d5:16 · qa
Why do neutrinos experience only the weak and gravitational forces?
They are leptons, so the strong force is excluded, and they are electrically neutral, so the electromagnetic force does not act on them either. This is why neutrinos pass through matter almost undisturbed.
d5:17 · qa
An unknown particle is observed to experience the strong nuclear force. What can you conclude about its composition?
It contains quarks, so it is a hadron — either a baryon (three quarks) or a meson (a quark–antiquark pair). It cannot be a lepton.

    Reversing the arrow turns the products back into the reactants, so a decay becomes a capture and an emission becomes an absorption. The same conserved quantities balance in either direction.
